## Abstract

Public administrative decisions combine product descriptions, attributed submissions, legal interpretation and an institutional outcome. Turning them into model inputs can create apparent evidence gaps or conceal choices behind reference answers. We develop a source-to-input audit for a bounded tariff-exclusion task, covering 73 case–candidate pairs from 67 US customs rulings, 208 inherited condition units and a 397-entry exclusion inventory. Records link objects and attributes to source spans, statement roles, admitted input versions and interpretation options. The audit distinguishes 132 direct-evidence units, including two nonbinding units, from 45 interpretive, 19 insufficient and 12 disputed units. Three source assertions restore four condition relations without resolving their whole cases. Among 58 cases with conjunctive structures, three declared interpretation packages yield 10, 17 and 19 supported cases. A qualified Qwen3-14B interface is tested on 108 constructed inputs and 96 linked relations. Direct decisions match 98/108 references and supplied-checklist decisions 104/108. The checklist matches 211/216 condition states and joint state-and-evidence references, but omits four necessary requests and makes one unsupported refutation. A semantic reference correction lowers condition agreement without changing whole-input answers. On eight source-restoration endpoints, direct and checklist decisions match seven and six references; each succeeds on three of four linked restorations. The semantic coding is AI-assisted and lacks independent customs-expert validation. The contribution is an inspectable evidence resource and recalculable evaluation protocol that separates input selection, interpretation and functional behavior, without estimating autonomous legal accuracy.

Keywords: legal artificial intelligence; evidence construction; customs rulings; tariff exclusions; evaluation validity; source provenance
